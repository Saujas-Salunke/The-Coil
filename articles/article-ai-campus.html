Scientists May Have Finally Discovered Why College Students Open 37 Tabs And Read None Of Them

A semester-long study of campus browser habits suggests the tab bar has quietly become a coping mechanism disguised as a workspace.

By Priya Nathanson Published September 18, 2026 Updated September 18, 2026 Reading time 6 min

The Coil is a fictional parody publication. All stories and quotes are created for demonstration purposes.

Every semester, somewhere between week three and the first round of midterms, a strange transformation occurs. A student who once opened a browser to look up one specific thing now opens it to look up thirty-seven specific things, none of which get read, several of which get bookmarked "for later," and at least one of which is a tab that has been open so long nobody remembers why it exists.

A research group based in the campus's Department of Behavioral Computing spent a full semester quietly logging browser activity across a volunteer sample of undergraduates. Their conclusion, delivered at a small and mostly unattended campus symposium last week, was blunt: the modern student's tab bar is not a workspace. It is a museum of intentions.

The tabs tell a story

Lead researcher Dr. Elaine Ferro described the data as "less about information retrieval and more about a very specific form of denial." According to the study, the average participant kept 34 to 41 tabs open at any given time, spanning categories that included lecture slides, a food delivery app, three near-identical shopping searches for the same pair of shoes, and one academic paper that had not been scrolled past the abstract in eleven days.

"At some point the tab isn't a task anymore. It's a promise you made to yourself in a moment of ambition," Dr. Ferro said.

Notably, the study found that closing a tab produced a measurable spike in anxiety among participants, even when the tab in question had not been interacted with in over a week. Researchers have tentatively labeled this the "premature closure effect," though they admit the term may be doing more narrative work than the data strictly supports.

Why this keeps happening

The research team's working theory is refreshingly unscientific: opening a tab feels like progress, even when it isn't. Each new tab represents a small commitment to eventually doing something, and the accumulation of these commitments creates the illusion of a very busy, very productive browser session — regardless of what actually gets done inside it.

Campus IT staff, who were consulted for the study's appendix, added that this behavior is not limited to laptops. "We've seen phones with encyclopedic tab counts too," said one technician who asked to remain unnamed "for reasons that will become clear if my own phone is ever subpoenaed."

What students had to say

When approached for comment, most students declined to discuss their tab counts on the record, though one junior, reached while visibly toggling between fourteen open windows, said only: "I'm aware. I'm handling it. Please don't tell my advisor."

Another participant defended the practice entirely. "It's not procrastination," they said. "It's optionality. I could read any of these at any time. That's power."

The recommendation nobody asked for

The study's final recommendation — close five tabs a day, no exceptions — has reportedly been followed by zero of the original participants. Dr. Ferro says her team is not discouraged. "Real behavioral change takes time," she said. "Also, to be transparent, several of us on the research team still have over 20 tabs open right now. We contain multitudes."

For now, The Coil can confirm only one certainty to come out of this research: somewhere on this campus, a browser is currently rendering a tab that was opened during syllabus week and has not been touched since.
