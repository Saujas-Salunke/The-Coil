This Family Drinks Tea Every Evening. The Reason Why Will Surprise You

One Indian family's daily tea ritual has baffled outsiders for years. Researchers have finally uncovered the mysterious truth.

By Renata Dubois Published September 20, 2026 Updated September 20, 2026 Reading time 4 min

The Coil is a fictional parody publication. All stories and quotes are created for demonstration purposes.

Every evening at around 5 PM, one family in Mumbai follows the same ritual. Someone makes tea. Everyone sits down. Someone says, "Just one cup." Someone else immediately asks for another.

The Sharma family has followed this tradition for twelve years, through two lockdowns, one pandemic, and approximately 4,380 biscuit purchases. Neighbors have speculated endlessly about hidden meanings, ancestral blessings, or secret ceremonies passed down through generations.

The investigation begins

Cultural anthropologists were eventually recruited to study the phenomenon. Dr. Priya Menendez, who specializes in domestic beverage rituals, spent three weeks observing the evening ceremony from a respectful distance.

"We approached this with open minds," said Menendez. "Some families practice meditation at this hour. Others gather for spiritual readings. There's even a documented case of one household watching cooking shows exclusively. We assumed the Sharmas had something equally profound."

"Day one: They drank tea. Day seven: Still drinking tea. Day twenty-one: I began questioning my career choices. But then—day thirty—we discovered the truth," recounted Menendez in her published findings.

A shocking discovery

Researchers investigating the phenomenon have discovered that there is no ancient secret behind it. They simply like tea.

The revelation sent shockwaves through the academic community. Several researchers questioned whether the finding was robust enough for peer review. "We spent weeks developing hypotheses about astrological timing, sugar intake optimization, and intergenerational bonding theory," said co-author Dr. Rajesh Kumar. "Turns out tea tastes good."

Veteran chai-wallahs near the Sharma residence offered their perspective. "It's normal," said one vendor who has served the family since 2014. "But Americans ask too many questions. In India, we drink chai. We do not write papers about chai."

The biscuit connection

The tradition continues today, with biscuits occasionally being sacrificed alongside it. Research indicates that Marie biscuits pair optimally with evening tea consumption, though digestive and Parle-G varieties receive occasional consideration during special occasions.

"The biscuit situation has evolved," explained Amit Sharma, family patriarch and chief cup refiller. "Initially it was just one per person. Now we have a biscuit plate that disappears mysteriously every evening. I think the dog is helping himself, but no one can prove anything."

Broader cultural implications

Sociologists suggest this case study may reshape understanding of everyday family traditions. Not every ritual requires deep philosophical justification. Sometimes people consume beverages they enjoy at consistent times of day.

Meanwhile, the Sharma family continues their evening gathering uninterrupted. Tea is made. Cups are poured. The phrase "just one cup" is uttered with predictable frequency. Biscuits are consumed in quantities that defy initial intentions.

For now, researchers are studying other household mysteries. Next on their agenda: why parents know where children are hiding snacks without looking, and the exact moment milk reaches boiling point when no one is watching the stove.
